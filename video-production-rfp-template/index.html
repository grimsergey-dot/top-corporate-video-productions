Recommendations

How to run a video production RFP

Each point is backed by what the public RFPs we read actually do.

  1. Publish the criteria and their weights.

    29 of the 49 RFPs we read publish points for each criterion; 20 only list factors. Weights tell studios where to put their effort and make your scoring defensible. A good starting point is the median formula: Experience & qualifications 25% · Approach & creative 25% · Portfolio / work samples 20% · Price 20% · References 10%.

  2. Keep price to about a fifth to a quarter of the score.

    The median RFP gives price 20% of the points. Video is a creative service: the RFPs that publish results show winners chosen on experience and approach, not on the lowest bid.

  3. State a budget range.

    Only 7 of 49 RFPs state a budget. Without one, proposals range from a phone shoot to a feature film and cannot be compared. A not-to-exceed figure lets studios compete on ideas inside your range.

  4. Define the scope precisely.

    List the number of videos, lengths, formats (16:9 and vertical 9:16 for social), who writes the script, shoot days, locations, revision rounds and captions. Identical scope is what makes prices comparable.

  5. Ask for proof, not free creative work.

    Ask for 3 references and similar work samples with links — the most common requirements. Only 9 of 49 require a creative concept up front; asking for an approach is fair, asking for free scripts or storyboards deters the best studios.

  6. Give three to five weeks and a questions deadline.

    The median RFP allows 24 days to respond, and 37 of 49 set a deadline for questions. Publish answers to all bidders as an addendum.

  7. Settle rights and delivery in the RFP.

    20 of 49 RFPs require the raw footage and project files. Say who owns the final videos and the footage, which files you receive, captions, and music and talent releases — it changes the price.

  8. Shortlist, then interview.

    Score written proposals first, then invite the top two or three studios to present. Many RFPs reserve points or a separate round for interviews.

Methodology

How we collected and read the RFPs

Sources. Public solicitation documents posted by US public bodies — cities, counties, state agencies, federal buyers, transit and port authorities, public universities and colleges, school districts, utilities and other special districts — found through their procurement pages and public bid boards, issued 2022–2026. We used only documents anyone can open without an account.

What qualified. Solicitations (RFP, RFQ or RFQual) where video production is the main subject: on-call video services, PSAs, recruitment, explainer, marketing and documentary videos, animation. We excluded live-streaming and meeting-broadcast operations, AV installations and broad marketing-agency RFPs where video is a single line. Every RFP had to state its evaluation or selection criteria.

What we extracted. For each RFP: buyer and type, dates and time to respond, contract type and term, stated budget, scope (videos, lengths, formats, revisions, captions, ownership of raw footage), each evaluation criterion with its points, what proposals must contain (work samples, references, page limits, insurance, interviews), and — where the buyer published them — the evaluation results: number of bidders, winner, prices.

How we computed the figures. Weights are each criterion's points divided by the RFP's total, grouped into comparable categories (experience, portfolio, approach, price, references, schedule, local or small-business preference). Medians are taken across the 29 RFPs that publish points; counts such as “asks for references” are out of all 49 RFPs and count only explicit requirements.

Limits. Public RFPs lean toward government buyers and on-call contracts; private companies run simpler processes. Evaluation results are published for a minority of RFPs, so winner and price findings rest on 13 cases. Each row in the table links to the original document so you can check it.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

A request for proposals is a formal document a company or public body sends to several video production companies. It describes the project — goals, audience, scope, budget, timeline — explains what proposals must contain and how they will be scored, and sets the deadline. Studios answer with an approach, a team, a schedule and a price.

Background and goals, audience, reference videos, scope of work (number of videos, lengths, formats, script, shoot days, revisions), where the video will be shown, budget range, tender timeline, what proposals must include, evaluation criteria with weights, and rights and delivery terms. The builder on this page covers all of them.

In the 29 public RFPs that publish points, the median formula is Experience & qualifications 25% · Approach & creative 25% · Portfolio / work samples 20% · Price 20% · References 10%. Experience, work samples and approach together usually outweigh price.

The median in our sample is 24 days. Three to five weeks is typical for a single video or an on-call contract; add time if you require a creative concept or interviews.
